0.1 Modular arithmetic

What is modulus

The operation \(a \bmod m\) returns the remainder of the integer division of \(a\) by \(m\).

\(17 \bmod 5 = 2 \qquad \text{because } 17 = 3 \cdot 5 + 2\)

\(-3 \bmod 13 = 10 \qquad \text{because } -3 + 13 = 10\)

Convention: in cryptography, results modulo \(q\) are always in the range \([0, q-1]\).

Arithmetic in \(\mathbb{Z}_q\)

We denote by \(\mathbb{Z}_q = \{0, 1, \ldots, q-1\}\) the set of integers modulo \(q\). In this set, addition, subtraction, and multiplication are always reduced modulo \(q\):

\(7 + 9 \equiv 3 \pmod{13}\)

\(3 \cdot 5 \equiv 2 \pmod{13}\)

Multiplicative inverse

In ordinary arithmetic, the inverse of a number serves to obtain \(1\) through multiplication (e.g., the inverse of \(5\) is \(\frac{1}{5}\), because \(5 \cdot \frac{1}{5} = 1\)). In modular arithmetic, fractions do not exist. The inverse of a number \(a\) must therefore be another integer \(b\) that, when multiplied by \(a\), gives a remainder of 1 modulo \(q\):

\(a \cdot b \equiv 1 \pmod{q}\)

The existence rule

The inverse of \(a\) does not always exist. It can be found if and only if \(a\) and \(q\) have no common divisors (i.e., if their Greatest Common Divisor is 1: \(\gcd(a, q) = 1\)).

  • Key property: If the modulus \(q\) is a prime number, every number in the set (except zero) is guaranteed to have an inverse.

Practical example: Computing \(5^{-1} \bmod 13\)

The notation \(5^{-1}\) means: "Find the inverse of 5 modulo 13." We seek a number \(b\) such that \(5 \cdot b\) gives a remainder of \(1\) when divided by \(13\):

  1. Try multiplying \(5\) by various integers.
  2. Choosing \(b = 8\), we get: \(5 \cdot 8 = 40\).
  3. Divide \(40\) by \(13\): 13 goes into 40 three times (\(13 \cdot 3 = 39\)) with a remainder of 1.

The answer is 8, because \(5 \cdot 8 = 40 \equiv 1 \pmod{13}\).

Modular exponentiation

Modular exponentiation consists of computing the remainder of a power: \(a^k \pmod m\). This operation is the backbone of modern cryptography (like the RSA algorithm). It is used because it is a "one-way clock": it is very easy to compute the result, but it is almost impossible to reverse the process (to discover the exponent \(k\) from the result).

The problem of large numbers

If we need to compute \(5^{11} \pmod{13}\), direct computation is impossible for a computer if the exponent has hundreds of digits. The number \(5^{11}\) would become too large for memory.

The solution: The Square-and-Multiply algorithm

This algorithm allows computing the result in very few steps (\(O(\log k)\) multiplications), always working with small numbers and preventing the power from growing indefinitely.

How it works in practice (Example: \(5^{11} \bmod 13\))

Convert the exponent to binary: \(11 = 8 + 2 + 1\). Instead of multiplying \(5\) by itself \(11\) times, we compute the squared powers step by step, immediately reducing the result modulo \(13\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Base: } & 5^1 \equiv 5 \pmod{13} \\ \text{Square: } & 5^2 = 25 \equiv 12 \pmod{13} \\ \text{Square of square: } & 5^4 = 12^2 = 144 \equiv 1 \pmod{13} \\ \text{Next square: } & 5^8 = 1^2 = 1 \pmod{13} \end{aligned} \]

Now we combine only the pieces that make up our exponent \(11\) (that is, \(5^8 \cdot 5^2 \cdot 5^1\)):

\(5^{11} = 5^8 \cdot 5^2 \cdot 5^1 \equiv 1 \cdot 12 \cdot 5 = 60 \pmod{13}\)

Divide \(60\) by \(13\): \(13\) goes into \(60\) \(4\) times (\(52\)) with a remainder of \(8\).

Final result: \(8\) (we did only 4 steps instead of 11).

0.2 Linear algebra: vectors and matrices

Vectors

A vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n\) is an ordered list of \(n\) integers computed modulo \(q\):

\(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{pmatrix}\)

In vector operations, every result is reduced modulo \(q\).

Practical example

With \(q = 13\):

\(\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 9 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 7 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\)

Vector addition

\(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 5+8 \\ 9+7 \\ 2+4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 13 \\ 16 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \pmod{13}\)

  • \(5+8=13 \equiv 0 \pmod{13}\)
  • \(9+7=16 \equiv 3 \pmod{13}\)
  • \(2+4=6 \equiv 6 \pmod{13}\)

Dot product

\(\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = (u_1 v_1) + (u_2 v_2) + (u_3 v_3) \pmod{13}\)

Calculation:

  • \(5 \cdot 8 = 40\)
  • \(9 \cdot 7 = 63\)
  • \(2 \cdot 4 = 8\)

Sum: \(40 + 63 + 8 = 111\)

Reduction modulo 13: \(111 \equiv 7 \pmod{13}\)

Matrices

A matrix \(A \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{m \times n}\) is an array of rows and columns.

(In practice, the matrix \(A\) in ML-KEM has dimensions \(k \times k\) with \(k = 3, 4\) or \(5\) and is generated from a random seed via SHAKE.)

Matrix-vector product

\((\mathbf{A} \mathbf{v})_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij} v_j \pmod{q}\)

Example

\(\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 9 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -7 \end{pmatrix} \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \pmod{13}\)

Detailed steps

First row: \((4 \cdot -1) + (7 \cdot 1) = 3\)

Second row: \((9 \cdot -1) + (2 \cdot 1) = -7\)

Reduction modulo 13: \(-7 + 13 = 6\)

Therefore: \(-7 \equiv 6 \pmod{13}\)

Infinity norm

\(\|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty = \max_i |v_i|\)

This norm measures the component with the maximum absolute value. In the context of MLWE, error vectors must have a very small norm.

Practical examples

Valid case: \(\mathbf{e} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\)

\(\|\mathbf{e}\|_\infty = 2\)

Invalid case: \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -7 \end{pmatrix}\)

\(\|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty = 7\)

0.3 Polynomials

Polynomials with coefficients in \(\mathbb{Z}_q\)

A polynomial in \(\mathbb{Z}_q[X]\) has the form:

\(f(X) = a_0 + a_1 X + a_2 X^2 + \cdots + a_{n-1} X^{n-1}\)

where each \(a_i \in \{0, 1, \ldots, q-1\}\).

Polynomial addition

Add the corresponding coefficients modulo \(q\):

\((f + g)(X) = (a_0 + b_0) + (a_1 + b_1)X + \cdots \pmod{q}\)

Polynomial multiplication and reduction

The product of two polynomials of degree \(\leq n-1\) can have degree up to \(2n-2\). To keep them compact, the result is reduced modulo a special polynomial.

In ML-KEM, \(X^n + 1\) is used with \(n = 256\). The reduction rule is:

\(X^{256} \equiv -1 \pmod{X^{256}+1}\)

So whenever \(X^{256}\) appears in the product, it is replaced with \(-1\).

Simple example

With \(n = 4\), \(q = 5\), modulo \(X^4+1\):

\(f = X^3 + 2, \quad g = X^2 + 1\)

\(f \cdot g = X^5 + X^3 + 2X^2 + 2\)

\(X^5 = X \cdot X^4 \equiv X \cdot (-1) = -X \pmod{X^4+1}\)

\(\Rightarrow f \cdot g \equiv -X + X^3 + 2X^2 + 2 \pmod{X^4+1, 5}\)

0.4 Algebraic structures: groups, rings, fields

Structure Operations Key properties
Group one (+) closure, associativity, identity, inverse
Ring two (+, ×) abelian group for +, semigroup for ×
Field two (+, ×) ring with multiplicative inverse for all ≠ 0

\(\mathbb{Z}_q\) as a field

If \(q\) is prime, \(\mathbb{Z}_q\) is a field: every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. In ML-KEM, \(q = 3329\) is prime → \(\mathbb{Z}_{3329}\) is a field.

The ring \(R_q\)

\(R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X] / (X^{256}+1)\)

It is a ring (not a field, because not all polynomials have an inverse), but it has useful properties for cryptography:

  • every polynomial has exactly 256 coefficients;
  • multiplication is efficient via NTT (see file 07);
  • the polynomial \(X^{256}+1\) is cyclotomic, which guarantees good security properties.
What does it mean that \(X^{256}+1\) is cyclotomic?

In algebra, a cyclotomic polynomial is a special polynomial whose roots are the primitive roots of unity (i.e., complex numbers that, when raised to a certain power, give \(1\)).

In the specific case of \(X^{256}+1\), this property guarantees three enormous advantages in cryptography (as in ML-KEM):

  • Irreducibility: The polynomial cannot be factored into smaller polynomials with integer coefficients. This prevents an attacker from "breaking" the mathematical problem into easier-to-solve parts.
  • Ultra-fast multiplication (NTT): It allows the use of the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT). Thanks to this algorithm, polynomial multiplication goes from a complexity of \(O(n^2)\) to just \(O(n \log n)\), making post-quantum cryptography incredibly fast.
  • Uniform security: It distributes the error (noise) in a perfectly symmetric way across all 256 coefficients during operations, eliminating structural weak points that hackers could exploit.

0.5 Cryptographic hash functions

A hash function \(H : \{0,1\}^* \to \{0,1\}^k\) maps arbitrary-length inputs to fixed-length outputs.

Required properties in cryptography:

  • Preimage resistance: given \(H(x)\), it is impossible to recover \(x\)
  • Collision resistance: finding \(x \neq y\) with \(H(x) = H(y)\) is computationally infeasible
  • Avalanche effect: changing one bit of input changes ~50% of the output

SHA-3 and SHAKE

ML-KEM exclusively uses primitives from the SHA-3 family (NIST standardized 2015):

Function Output Use in ML-KEM
SHA3-256 fixed 256 bits general hashing
SHA3-512 fixed 512 bits key hashing
SHAKE128 variable length generation of \(A\) (XOF)
SHAKE256 variable length key derivation

SHAKE is an XOF (eXtendable Output Function): it produces as many bytes as desired from a given input. In ML-KEM, SHAKE128(seed) generates the entire public matrix \(A\) from a 32-byte seed.

0.6 Probability and noise distribution

Centered binomial distribution \(B_\eta\)

In ML-KEM, the noise is not Gaussian but centered binomial. The sampling works as follows:

  1. Sample \(2\eta\) uniform bits: \(a_1, \ldots, a_\eta, b_1, \ldots, b_\eta \in \{0,1\}\)
  2. Return \(\sum a_i - \sum b_i\)

The result is an integer in \([-\eta, +\eta]\) with an approximately Gaussian distribution centered at 0.

Why not true Gaussian? The binomial distribution is simpler to sample in a constant-time manner (resistant to side-channel attacks), and the security is similar.

For ML-KEM-768: \(\eta_1 = 2, \eta_2 = 2\). Noise values will be in \(\{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\}\).

Negligibility

A function \(f(\lambda)\) is negligible if for every polynomial \(p(\cdot)\) there exists \(\lambda_0\) such that for every \(\lambda > \lambda_0\):

\(f(\lambda) < \frac{1}{p(\lambda)}\)

In practice: it decreases faster than any polynomial inverse. Error probabilities in ML-KEM are negligible in the security parameter \(\lambda\).

0.7 Computational complexity

Notations

  • \(O(f(n))\): asymptotic upper bound (order of magnitude)
  • \(\Omega(f(n))\): lower bound
  • \(\Theta(f(n))\): tight bound

Relevant complexity classes

Class Meaning Examples
P Solvable in polynomial time multiplication, sorting
NP Verifiable in polynomial time SAT, TSP
NP-hard At least as hard as NP factoring (presumably)

Cryptographic problems and their difficulty

Problem Classical difficulty Quantum difficulty
