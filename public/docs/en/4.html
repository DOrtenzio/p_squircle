4.1 Definition of a lattice

Formal definition

A lattice \(\mathcal{L}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) is the set of all integer linear combinations of \(n\) linearly independent vectors \(\mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_n \in \mathbb{R}^m\):

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_n) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i \mathbf{b}_i \;\middle|\; z_i \in \mathbb{Z} \right\} \]

The vectors \(\mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_n\) form a basis of the lattice.

Geometric intuition

In 2D, imagine an infinite grid of points. Each point is reachable by the integer (not real) combination of two basis vectors:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \left\{ a \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + b \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \;\middle|\; a, b \in \mathbb{Z} \right\} \]

This is the standard integer grid \(\mathbb{Z}^2\).

But the same grid has infinitely many different bases:

\[ \mathbf{b}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

generates the same lattice. Some bases are "good" (short and nearly orthogonal vectors), others are "bad" (long and nearly parallel vectors).

Dimension and rank

  • Ambient dimension \(m\): number of coordinates of each vector
  • Rank \(n\): number of vectors in the basis

If \(n = m\): full-rank lattice

In cryptography we typically use very large \(n = m\) lattices (256–4096).

4.2 Hard problems on lattices

SVP – Shortest Vector Problem

Given: a basis \(B\) of a lattice \(\mathcal{L}\)
Find: the shortest non-zero vector in \(\mathcal{L}\)

\[ \text{SVP}: \quad \text{find } \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{L} \setminus \{0\} \text{ such that } \|\mathbf{v}\| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\| \; \forall \mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{L} \setminus \{0\} \]

In 2D it's easy (Gauss's algorithm). In high dimension (hundreds), it is NP-hard under certain assumptions, and the best known algorithm requires exponential time.

CVP – Closest Vector Problem

Given: a basis \(B\) of \(\mathcal{L}\) and a target point \(\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^m\) (not in the lattice)
Find: the lattice point closest to \(\mathbf{t}\)

\[ \text{CVP}: \quad \text{find } \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{L} \text{ such that } \|\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{v}\| \leq \|\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{u}\| \; \forall \mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{L} \]

CVP is at least as hard as SVP. It is at the heart of decapsulation in ML-KEM: those who know the secret \(\mathbf{s}\) can solve the corresponding "easy" CVP, those who don't know it cannot.

GapSVP and SIS

There are approximate variants (GapSVP) and related problems (SIS – Short Integer Solution) that form the basis of security proofs for lattice-based cryptography. For our purposes, it is sufficient to know that they all reduce to one another, forming a family of hard problems.

4.3 From SVP to LWE

How do you build a cryptographic system from SVP?

Regev's idea (2005): instead of working directly with SVP (geometrically intuitive but awkward to use), define an equivalent statistical problem: Learning With Errors (LWE).

The security reduction demonstrates that:

If you break LWE ⇒ You solve GapSVP

Therefore LWE is at least as hard as GapSVP in high dimension.

4.4 LWE: Learning With Errors

Definition

Fix parameters \(n\) (dimension), \(q\) (modulus), \(\chi\) (error distribution):

LWE distribution: choose secret \(\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n\). Generate samples:

\[ (\mathbf{a},\; b = \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e \bmod q) \]

where \(\mathbf{a} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n\) uniform, \(e \leftarrow \chi\) small.

Decision LWE problem: distinguish samples \((\mathbf{a}, b)\) from uniform random pairs \((\mathbf{a}, u)\) with \(u \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q\).

Search LWE problem: given a set of samples \((\mathbf{a}_i, b_i)\), find \(\mathbf{s}\).

LWE parameters

Parameter Symbol Role
Dimension \(n\) Security grows exponentially in \(n\)
Modulus \(q\) Must be large enough to contain the noise
Error distribution \(\chi\) Discrete Gaussian or centered binomial
Noise-to-signal ratio \(\alpha = \sigma/q\) Must be small but not too small

Example with n=2, q=7

Secret: \(\mathbf{s} = (3, 1)^T\)

LWE samples with small errors in \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\):

The vector a b = ⟨a, s⟩ + e mod 7 e
\((1, 2)\) \(1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 1 + 0 = 5\) 0
\((4, 3)\) \(4 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 1 + 1 = 16 \equiv 2\) +1
\((2, 6)\) \(2 \cdot 3 + 6 \cdot 1 + (-1) = 17 \equiv 3\) -1

Given the system with errors, finding \(\mathbf{s}\) is difficult: you can't use Gaussian elimination because the \(b_i\) values are not exact.

4.5 RLWE: ring-based variant

Standard LWE uses vectors. Ring-LWE (RLWE) moves operations to the polynomial ring:

\[ R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[X]/(X^n + 1) \]

In RLWE:

  • \(\mathbf{s} \in R_q\) (a polynomial, not a vector of integers)
  • \(a \in R_q\) (sampled uniformly)
  • \(b = a \cdot s + e \in R_q\)

Advantages of RLWE over LWE:

  • Key size: \(O(n)\) instead of \(O(n^2)\)
  • Speed: polynomial multiplication with NTT is \(O(n \log n)\) instead of \(O(n^2)\)

Disadvantage: additional algebraic structure → potentially more vulnerable; the security proof is slightly weaker.

4.6 MLWE: the basis of ML‑KEM

Module-LWE (MLWE) is a compromise between LWE and RLWE: it uses modules of polynomials (matrices of polynomials in \(R_q\)).

\[ \mathbf{A} \in R_q^{k \times k}, \quad \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{e} \in R_q^k \]
\[ \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e} \in R_q^k \]

The parameter \(k\) is the module dimension:

  • \(k = 2\) for ML-KEM-512
  • \(k = 3\) for ML-KEM-768
  • \(k = 4\) for ML-KEM-1024

Why MLWE?

Scheme Key size Speed Security
LWE \(O(n^2)\) → large slow more conservative
RLWE \(O(n)\) → small fast extra structure
MLWE \(O(k \cdot n)\) → medium fast balanced

MLWE allows scaling security by varying \(k\) without changing \(n\), while maintaining the optimizations of the ring structure.

4.7 Post‑quantum security of lattices

Best classical algorithm: BKZ (Block Korkine-Zolotarev)

The BKZ algorithm and its variants (BKZ 2.0, G6K) are the best classical algorithms for attacking lattices. Their complexity for ML-KEM-768 is estimated at \(> 2^{178}\) operations.

Impact of quantum computers

The best quantum algorithms for attacking MLWE (including QRAM-based approaches) offer at most a modest speedup — at most quadratic, keeping the problem's difficulty in the exponential regime.

This contrasts sharply with Shor, which offers an exponential speedup on factorization and DLP.

Why MLWE has no "Shor's algorithm"

MLWE does not have the abelian group structure that Shor exploits. The problem is fundamentally different: it is not a matter of finding periodicity in a function, but of finding a short vector in a high-dimensional lattice — a geometric problem for which no known quantum technique offers an exponential speedup.
