03 – Shor's Algorithm

Why RSA and ECC are vulnerable to quantum computers

3.1 Quantum computers: the essential basics

Qubit vs. classical bits

A classical bit is either 0 or 1. A qubit can be in superposition: a linear combination of \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\).

\[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 \]

Before measurement, the qubit is "both 0 and 1". After measurement, it collapses to 0 (with probability \(|\alpha|^2\)) or 1 (with probability \(|\beta|^2\)).

Analogy: imagine flipping a coin that, while in the air, represents both heads and tails simultaneously. Only when you look at it does it become one of the two.

Entanglement and interference

  • Entanglement: two qubits can be correlated such that measuring one instantly determines the other.
  • Quantum interference: the "wrong" computation paths cancel constructively (like waves that cancel out), while the "right" paths are amplified.

Quantum algorithms leverage interference to amplify correct answers and suppress wrong ones.

Quantum parallelism

With \(n\) qubits, one can represent a superposition of \(2^n\) states simultaneously. But measurement returns only one state → the trick is to build algorithms where interference leaves only the right answer to survive.

3.2 Shor's algorithm

Algorithm scheme for factoring \(n\)

  1. Input: composite integer \(n\)
  2. Initialization: Choose random \(a\) such that \(1 < a < n\) and \(\gcd(a,n)=1\)
  3. Quantum Preparation: Prepare a register in uniform superposition from \(0\) to \(2^k-1\)
  4. Oracle: Compute \(f(x) = a^x \bmod n\) in parallel (superposition)
  5. QFT: Apply the Quantum Fourier Transform to the first register
  6. Measurement: Measure the register to obtain a value correlated with the period \(r\)
  7. Classical: Use the continued fractions algorithm to estimate \(r\)
  8. Verification: If \(r\) is even and \(a^{r/2} \not\equiv -1 \pmod n\):
    • Compute \(\gcd(a^{r/2} - 1, n)\) and \(\gcd(a^{r/2} + 1, n)\)
    • These are non-trivial factors of \(n\)
  9. Loop: If it fails, go back to step 2 with a new \(a\)

Note: The quantum core (steps 3-5) finds the period \(r\) of the function \(f(x)\) in polynomial time, leveraging the constructive/destructive interference of the QFT.

3.3 Impact on RSA, DH, ECC

Scheme Security based on Vulnerable to Shor?
RSA Factorization Yes
DH (mod \(p\)) Discrete logarithm in \(\mathbb{Z}_p^*\) Yes
ECDH / ECDSA ECDLP (discrete logarithm on EC) Yes
ML-KEM (MLWE) Lattice problems No known efficient algorithm
AES-256 Pseudorandom permutations Only Grover (see below)

Shor solves any structured problem like finding periodicity in an abelian group. Factorization and discrete logarithm (including on EC) have exactly this structure. Lattice problems do not.

3.4 Grover: the threat to symmetric cryptography

Grover's algorithm (1996) offers a quadratic speedup in unstructured search:

  • Finding a key among \(2^k\) possibilities classically: \(O(2^k)\) attempts
  • With Grover: \(O(2^{k/2})\) attempts

Practical consequences:

Key Classical security Post-quantum security (Grover)
AES-128 128 bits 64 bits → insufficient
AES-256 256 bits 128 bits → still secure
SHA-256 256 bits 128 bits → secure
SHA3-256 256 bits 128 bits → secure

This is why NIST recommends AES-256 (not AES-128) in the post-quantum context.

3.5 State of the art of quantum computers (2024–2025)

Current quantum computers are NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum): they have tens to hundreds of physical qubits but with a high error rate.

To break RSA-2048 with Shor you need:

  • ~4000 perfect logical qubits
  • or ~4 million physical qubits with quantum error correction

The best current systems (Google Willow, IBM Heron, etc.) have ~100-1000 high-fidelity physical qubits, but we are still far from the required scale.

NIST estimate: cryptographically relevant quantum computers could exist within 10–15 years, with significant uncertainty in both directions.

3.6 Harvest Now, Decrypt Later

Even if quantum computers do not yet exist in cryptographically relevant form, there is a concrete threat today:

An adversary can harvest now encrypted communications (even if they cannot decrypt them), and decrypt them in the future when they have a sufficiently powerful quantum computer.

This attack is known as "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later" (HNDL) or store-now-decrypt-later.

Who is at risk:

  • Diplomatic and military communications with long secrecy requirements
  • Medical, financial, legal data with decades-long confidentiality requirements
  • Industrial secrets and IP

When to migrate?

If your data must remain secret for more years than it will take for sufficiently powerful quantum computers to exist, you need to migrate now.

This is the main reason why NIST standardized ML-KEM (FIPS 203) in 2024, and why organizations like NSA, ENISA, and ANSSI recommend immediate migration for high-criticality systems.
